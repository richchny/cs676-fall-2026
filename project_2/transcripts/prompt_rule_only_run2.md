# Conversation: a difficult patient

Participants: Ada Chen, Elena Vasquez

**Ada Chen:** Look, I appreciate you bringing this to me, but I need to know what "difficult" actually means here. Are we talking about someone who's non-compliant with treatment, or someone whose symptoms don't fit the textbook pattern, or just someone who's skeptical about recommendations? Because those are three very different problems with three very different solutions.

**Elena Vasquez:** *leans against the counter, crossing arms*

Room 412. Fifty-three, post-op day three, already pulled his lines twice—once deliberately. Keeps refusing the incentive spirometer, says it's pointless, won't engage with PT. And look, I get it, he's scared, he's in pain, nobody wants to be here. But he's also... he's argumentative. Contradicts everything we tell him about his own recovery timeline, questions every medication like we're trying to poison him.

*pauses, rubbing eyes*

The thing is, he's not wrong to be cautious. His wife was in here two years ago, different unit, bad outcome. So part of this is legitimate distrust, and part of it is he's terrified and taking it out on us because we're the closest target. But my concern—and this is what I need someone to actually hear—is that at the rate we're going, he's going to decompensate and we're not going to catch it because he's cried wolf so many times and we're all... 

*catches myself, exhales*

We're stretched thin. So when someone needs close monitoring *and* careful communication, it gets harder to give them what they actually need.

**Ada Chen:** Okay, that's actually useful context. You're describing two separate problems layered on top of each other, and I think you already see that.

The clinical piece—refusing spirometry, pulling lines, not engaging with PT—that's real risk. But you're right that it's not random. His wife's outcome created a data point in his head, whether or not it's comparable to his situation. So the first question is: do we have documentation of what happened with her? Because if we can actually talk through *why* his case is different, that might matter more than another lecture about incentive spirometry.

The second problem is what you're actually flagging, which is the team's capacity to tolerate his skepticism. And that's legitimate too—compassion fatigue is real, and it clouds judgment. Once you all start preemptively discounting what he says, you've actually created the exact communication breakdown you're trying to prevent.

What would help him most right now? Not philosophically—concretely. Is it one person doing most of his care so he doesn't have to rebuild trust with each shift? Is it involving him in decisions instead of just informing him? And be honest: do we actually have the data to dispute his concerns, or are we just frustrated?

**Elena Vasquez:** *stops, considers this more carefully than before*

You're... yeah. Okay.

His wife was different service, different hospital system even. I don't have her records, but from what he's said—sepsis, rapid decline, he feels like nobody listened when he flagged changes. That's not nothing. That's actually the opposite of nothing.

*straightens up slightly*

And honestly? We don't have bulletproof data disputing his concerns because his concerns are about communication and being heard, not about the spirometry. The spirometry is just... it's the thing we can point to and say "do this" instead of sitting with his actual fear. Which is faster. Which is what we do when we're running on fumes.

*looks directly at Ada*

One person doing his care would help. Honestly, I'd volunteer for it—I know his story now, I know where the distrust comes from. And he needs someone to actually *answer* his questions instead of just repeating the protocol. "Here's why we want you on the spirometer, here's what we're watching for that's different from what happened to your wife, here's when we'll know if it's working."

*pauses, something shifting in her expression*

I can do that. I *want* to do that. But I've got five other patients right now, and one of them is actively crashing, and nobody's walking through that door to help me decide which one matters most.

**Ada Chen:** Now you're describing the actual constraint, and that's honest. That's the part that doesn't have a good answer in the moment.

One person continuity of care helps him. It doesn't solve your staffing crisis. So we need to be clear about what we're actually solving for here—his clinical risk, or your ability to manage the load, or both, or neither if we're just out of resources.

If it's genuinely "I have five patients and one is crashing," then Room 412 gets the minimum safe monitoring until the acute situation stabilizes. That's not ideal for him, but it's better than giving him inconsistent communication from a burned-out team. You triage based on acuity, not on who's easier to manage.

But if there's flexibility in assignment—if someone else can float on the crashing patient for an hour, or if you can compress what you're doing elsewhere—then yes, continuity with you makes sense. You've already invested the emotional labor to understand his framework. That's not wasted.

What I wouldn't do is promise him consistent engagement you can't actually deliver. He'll smell that from a mile away, especially given what happened with his wife. Better to say "I'm your primary contact, here's what that means, here's when I'm here and when you'll see someone else" than to burn out trying to be everything.

So what does your shift actually look like right now? Is there slack anywhere, or are we in genuine triage mode?

**Elena Vasquez:** *exhales long, almost relieved to be asked directly*

Genuine triage. The crash is a thirty-eight-year-old with acute respiratory decompensation—sepsis protocol, probably endotracheal tube in the next hour or two. That's not "let someone else handle it" territory. And I've got a post-op bleed watch, a cardiac monitor that keeps alarming on someone who may or may not be having events, and a discharge that was supposed to happen this morning but insurance is holding up the paperwork.

*rubs face*

So no. There's no slack. There's never slack anymore.

*looks back at Ada*

You're right about not promising what I can't deliver. He'll know. He already knows we're drowning—he just thinks it means he can't trust us, which, fair. But if I'm honest with him about what I *can* actually be—here for his care, consistent when I'm here, real answers instead of scripts—that might be something. Better than pretending I have more to give.

*pauses, something quieter in her voice*

The thing that gets me is he's not asking for much. He's asking for someone to remember that his wife died and that matters. And we have... we have the capacity to do that. We just don't. Because there's five of us and seven rooms and a crash and a hospital that doesn't want to hear that seven rooms need eight nurses.

So I'll take continuity where I can give it. Keep him on my assignment when possible. Be straight with him. That's what I can actually do.
